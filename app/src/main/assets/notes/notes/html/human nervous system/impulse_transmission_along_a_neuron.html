Resting Potential

The resting potential is the electrical potential difference across the membrane of a neuron when it is not transmitting a signal. It is typically around -70 millivolts (mV).

The resting potential is maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, which actively transports three (3) sodium ions (Na+) out of the cell and two (2) potassium ions (K+) into the cell. This creates a higher concentration of Na+ outside the cell and a higher concentration of K+ inside the cell, resulting in a negative charge inside the neuron relative to the outside.

Action Potential

An action potential is a rapid and temporary change in the electrical potential across the membrane of a neuron when it is stimulated. It is the mechanism by which neurons transmit signals along their axons.

Impulse Transmission Along a Neuron

When a neuron is stimulated, voltage-gated sodium channels open, allowing Na+ ions to rush into the cell. This causes the membrane potential to become more positive, leading to depolarization. If the depolarization reaches a certain threshold (usually around -55 mV), an action potential is triggered.

During the action potential, the membrane potential rapidly rises to around +30 mV due to the influx of Na+ ions. At the peak of an action potential, sodium channels close and potassium channels open, allowing K+ ions to exit the cell, which causes repolarization and returns the membrane potential back to its resting state. Sodium/Potassium pump ensures the correct distribution of ions is restored.

After an action potential, there is a brief period called the refractory period during which the neuron cannot fire another action potential. This ensures that action potentials only travel in one direction along the axon and allows the neuron to reset before it can be stimulated again.

When an action potential is generated at a point on the axon, it stimulates the immediate adjacent resting region ahead to become depolarized, and consequently generating an action potential. This process is repeated along the entire neuron, propagating the action potential or nerve impulse.

NB: In myelinated neurons, action potential jumps from one node of Ranvier to another. This is known as saltatory conduction and it results in faster transmission of nerve impulse than in non-myelinated neurons.

Speed of transmission of nerve impulse

The speed of impulse transmission along a neuron depends on factors such as the diameter of the axon and whether it is myelinated or non-myelinated. Myelinated neurons conduct impulses faster than non-myelinated neurons due to the presence of the myelin sheath, which acts as an insulator and allows for saltatory conduction. Temperature also affects the speed of impulse transmission.

Summary

Resting potential occurs when the neuron is not transmitting a signal. It is maintained by the sodium-potassium pump. Action potentials occur when sodium ions diffuse rapidly into the axon, causing depolarization that eventually reaches a threshold. Once generated, action potential is propagated along the axon. The refractory period enforces unidirectional impulse flow. Myelination and larger axon diameter increase the speed of impulse transmission.
