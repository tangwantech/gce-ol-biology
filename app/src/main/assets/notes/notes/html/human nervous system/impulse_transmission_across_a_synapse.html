Impulse Transmission Across a Synapse

Synapse is the junction between neurons.It allows communication between neurons. A special type of synapse known as neuromuscular junction exits where the axon terminal of a motor neuron comes close to the membrane of a muscle.

When a nerve impulse arrives the synaptic knob, a neurotransmitter e.g. acetylcholine, is discharged from the synaptic vesicles into the synaptic cleft. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to specific receptors on the post-synaptic membrane. The membrane becomes depolarised. When a certain threshold is reached, an action potential is generated and propagated in the post-synaptic neuron.

After acetylcholine has carried out its function, an enzyme called cholinesterase destroys it preventing continuous stimulation of the post-synaptic neuron. Transmission of nerve impulse is electrical along the axon and chemical across the synapse.

NB: A nerve impulse is an electrochemical charge or wave of depolarization and repolarization that moves along a neuron.

Features of a nerve impulse

All-or-none law: A nerve impulse is either generated or not generated. If the stimulus is strong enough to reach the threshold, an action potential will be generated. If the stimulus is below the threshold, no action potential will be generated.

Unidirectional: A nerve impulse travels in one direction along a neuron, from the dendrites to the axon terminal. This is due to the refractory period that follows an action potential, which prevents the impulse from traveling backward.

Non-decremental: A nerve impulse does not decrease in strength as it travels along the neuron. The action potential is regenerated at each point along the axon, ensuring that the signal remains strong until it reaches its destination.

All impulses are the same: The strength of a nerve impulse is not determined by the strength of the stimulus. All action potentials have the same magnitude, regardless of the intensity of the stimulus. The frequency of action potentials can vary to encode information about the strength of the stimulus.

Reasons for unidirectional transmission of nerve impulses

1. Neurotransmitters are released only from the pre-synaptic neuron and not from the post-synaptic neuron.

2. Receptors for neurotransmitters are located only on the post-synaptic membrane and not on the pre-synaptic membrane.

3. The refractory period prevents the nerve impulse from traveling backward along the axon.

Summary

At a synapse, a nerve impulse is transmitted chemically by neurotransmitter released by synaptic vesicles in the presynaptic terminal. The neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the post-synaptic membrane, converting the chemical signal back into an electrical signal. Enzymes such as cholinesterase stop stimulation after transmission, and the process remains unidirectional because only the presynaptic neuron releases neurotransmitters and the receptors are located on the post-synaptic membrane. A nerve impulse is based on all-or-none principle, meaning it is either generated fully or not at all. Once generated, it travels along the axon without losing its strength.
