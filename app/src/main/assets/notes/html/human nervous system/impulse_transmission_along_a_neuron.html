Resting Potential

  • The resting potential is the electrical potential difference across the membrane of a neuron when it is not transmitting a signal. It is typically around -70 millivolts (mV).
  • During a resting potential, the outside of the cell is positively charged while the inside is negatively charged.
  • The resting potential is maintained by the sodium-potassium pump, which actively transports 3 Na+ ions out of the cell and 2 K+ ions into the cell, creating a concentration gradient and an electrical gradient across the membrane.

Action Potential

An action potential is a rapid and temporary change in the electrical potential across the membrane of a neuron when it is stimulated. It is the mechanism by which neurons transmit signals along their axons.

Impulse Transmission Along a Neuron

  • A nerve impulse is an electrochemical charge or wave of action potentials that moves along a neuron.
  • When a neuron is stimulated, voltage-gated sodium channels open, allowing Na+ ions to rush into the cell by diffusion. This causes the membrane potential to become more positive, leading to depolarization.
  • When depolarization reaches a certain threshold (usually around -55 mV), an action potential is generated.
  • The generation of an action potential at a point on the axon sets up a local current that stimulates the immediate adjacent resting region ahead to become depolarized, and subsequently generating an action potential. This process is repeated along the neuron, propagating the action potential or nerve impulse.
  • In myelinated neurons, action potential jumps from one node of Ranvier to another, a process known as saltatory conduction. This results in faster transmission of nerve impulses than in non-myelinated neurons.
  • After an action potential has occurred, the membrane is repolarized by the opening of voltage-gated potassium channels, allowing K+ ions to exit the cell by diffusion. This restores the resting potential.
  • Sodium potassium pump continues to function, actively transporting Na+ ions out of the cell and K+ ions into the cell, maintaining the resting potential.

NB: After an action potential, the neuron enters a refractory period during which it cannot fire another action potential. This ensures that action potentials only travel in one direction along the axon.

Features of a nerve impulse

  • A nerve impulse obeys the all-or-none law. If the stimulus is strong enough to reach the threshold, an action potential will be generated. If the stimulus is below the threshold, no action potential will be generated.
  • A nerve impulse is unidirectional, traveling from the dendrites to the axon terminal.
  • A nerve impulse is non-decremental, meaning it does not decrease in strength as it travels along the neuron.
  • All impulses are the same in magnitude, regardless of the intensity of the stimulus. The frequency of action potentials is the code for the strength of the stimulus.

Factors affecting the speed of transmission of nerve impulse

  • The larger the diameter of the axon, the faster the nerve impulse is transmitted. This is because larger diameter axons have less resistance to the flow of ions, allowing for faster transmission of nerve impulses.
  • Myelinated neurons conduct impulses faster than non-myelinated neurons. Myelin sheath acts as an insulator and allows for saltatory conduction, where the action potential jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next, increasing the speed of transmission.
  • Higher temperatures can increase the speed of nerve impulse transmission, while lower temperatures can slow it down.

Summary

When a neuron is not stimulated, it maintains a resting potential of around -70mV. When a stimulus is strong enough to depolarize the membrane to the threshold level, an action potential is generated. The action potential propagates along the axon as a nerve impulse, with the speed of transmission influenced by factors such as axon diameter, myelination, and temperature. After an action potential, the neuron enters a refractory period during which it cannot fire another action potential, ensuring that impulses only travel in one direction along the axon. Myelinated neurons conduct impulses faster than non-myelinated neurons due to saltatory conduction.
