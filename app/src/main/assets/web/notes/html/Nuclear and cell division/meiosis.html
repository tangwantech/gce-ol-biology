Types of Cell Division

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Describe the process of Meiosis.
  • State the sites of meiosis in plants and animals
  • State the significance of meiosis

2. Meiosis

  • Meiosis is the process by which a diploid cell divides to produce four haploid cells which are genetically different from each other as well as from the parent cell.

Stages of Meiosis

Meiosis is divided into Meiosis I and Meiosis II.

Meiosis I

The main purpose of meiosis I is to separate homologous chromosomes. It involves the following stages:

Interphase

  • DNA replicates and the diploid number of chromosomes doubles (becomes tetraploid)
  • Cell organelles duplicate
  • ATP is accumulated to power the process till the end.
  • The cell grows in size.
  • Centrioles duplicate in animal cells.

    • Interphase

Prophase I

  • Chromatin threads condense, becoming shorter, thicker and visible.
  • Each chromosome is seen to consists of two chromatids joined at the centromere.
  • In animal cell, centrioles move to opposite poles of the cell and form the spindle fibers.
  • Nucleolus disappears and nuclear membrane breaks down.
  • Homologous chromosomes pair up to form bivalents (tetrads) in a process called synapsis. Each bivalent consists of four chromatids.
  • Crossing over occurs between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes. This results in exchange of genetic material between the two chromatids and leads to genetic variation.

    • Prophase I

Metaphase I

  • Bivalents align at the cell's equator (metaphase plate), forming a double row.
  • Each chromosome of a bivalent is attached by the centromere to spindle fibers from opposite poles.

    • Metaphase I

Anaphase I

  • Spindle fibers shorten and pull the homologous chromosomes apart, and towards the opposite poles of the cell.
  • Chromatids remain attached at the centromere (centromeres do not divide).

    • Anaphase I

Telophase I

  • Chromosomes reach the opposite poles and become invisible chromatin.
  • Nuclear membrane reforms around each set of chromosomes.
  • Nucleolus reappears.
  • Spindle fibers disappear.

    • Telophase I

Cytokinesis I

  • In animal cells, the cell membrane pinches inwards to form a cleavage furrow along the equator of the cell. The furrow deepens until the cell is pinched into two new daughtern cells. Each daughter cell contains only one set of chromosomes and so can be described as haploid.
  • In plant cells, a cell plate forms across the equator of the cell from the center outwards. The cell plate is formed by fusion of vesicles form Golgi bodies, containing cellulose and other materials. The cell plate eventually develops into a new cell wall, separating the two haploid daughter cells.

    • Cytokinesis I

Meiosis II

Meiosis II is similar to mitosis. It main purpose is to separate sister chromatids. It involves the following stages:

Prophase II

  • Chromatin threads condense, becoming shorter, thicker and visible.
  • Each chromosome is seen to consists of two chromatids joined at the centromere.
  • In animal cell, centrioles move to opposite poles of the cell and form the spindle fibers.
  • Nucleolus disappears and nuclear membrane breaks down.

    • Prophase II

Metaphase II

  • Chromosomes align at the cell's equator (metaphase plate), forming a single row.
  • Each chromosome is attached to spindle fibers from opposite poles.

    • Metaphase II

Anaphase II

  • Centromere splits, spindle fibers shorten and pull the chromatids (now called chromosomes) apart and towards opposite poles, with centromeres leading.

    • Anaphase II

Telophase II

  • Chromosomes reach the opposite poles and become invisible chromatin.
  • Nuclear membrane reforms around each set of chromosomes.
  • Nucleolus reappears.
  • Spindle fibers disappear.

    • Telophase II

Cytokinesis II

  • Cytokinesis II results in the formation of four haploid daughter cells.
  • In animal cells, the cell membrane pinches inwards to form a cleavage furrow along the equator of the cell. The furrow deepens until the cell is pinched into two new daughtern cells. Each daughter cell contains only one set of chromosomes(haploid).

    • Cytokinesis II

Site of meiosis in plants and animals

  • In animals, meiosis occurs in the gonads (testes and ovaries) to produce gametes (sperm and egg).
  • In plants, meiosis occurs in the anthers of flowers to produce pollen grains and in the ovules to produce egg cells.

Significance of meiosis

  • Meiosis ensures genetic diversity by creating unique combinations of chromosomes in gametes through independent assortment and crossing over.
  • Meiosis prevents doubling of chromosome number in offspring of organisms that reproduce sexually.
  • Meiosis is important in sexual reproduction by producing gametes.

Summary

Meiosis is a type of cell division that reduces the chromosome number by half, producing four genetically distinct haploid cells. It involves two sequential divisions, Meiosis I and Meiosis II, each with distinct stages.

The main purpose of meiosis I is to separate homologous chromosomes, while meiosis II separates sister chromatids.

Meiosis occurs in the gonads of animals (testes and ovaries) and in the anthers and ovules of plants, playing a crucial role in sexual reproduction, genetic diversity, and maintaining chromosome number across generations.
