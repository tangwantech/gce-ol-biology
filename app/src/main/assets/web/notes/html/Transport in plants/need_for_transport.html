Notion of need for transport in plants

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Explain why multicellular organisms need a transport system
  • State the main transport tissues in plants and their functions
  • Differentiate between xylem and phloem

Introduction

Transport is the process of moving substances from one place to another. It can be from the environment to the organism and vice versa or within the organism.

Transport in unicellular organisms is by simple diffusion because these organisms have a small body size and their entire body (cell) is in direct contact with the surrounding environment, so materials can rapidly enter and leave the cell by simple diffusion.

In contrast, multicellular organisms are larger and have cells located deep inside the body, far from the external environment. Diffusion alone would be too slow and inefficient to transport materials over these long distances to meet the metabolic needs of all cells. Hence, they need a transport system

For a example plants need a transport system to:

  • Carry water and mineral salts from the soil to the leaves for photosynthesis.
  • Carry manufactured food substances from the leaves to other parts of the plant for use or storage.

How are oxygen and carbon dioxide transported in plants?

The transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide in plants occurs through the process of diffusion. Oxygen is produced during photosynthesis and diffuses out of the plant through stomata, while carbon dioxide enters the plant through stomata and diffuses to the cells where it is used in photosynthesis.

Transport tissues in plants (Vascular tissues)

There are two main types of transport tissues in plants namely: xylem and phloem. Both tissues are also described as compound tissues because they are composed of multiple cell types working together to perform their functions.

Xylem

  • Xylem is made up of four types of cells: tracheids, vessel elements, fibers, and parenchyma cells.
  • The main conducting cells are the tracheids and vessel elements.
  • Fibers provide structural support to the xylem tissue.
  • Parenchyma cells are involved in the storage and gaseous exchange.
  • Tracheids and vessel elements are dead at maturity, while other cells in the xylem are living.

Functions of xylem

  • Transport of water and minerals from the roots to the leaves
  • Provide mechanical support to the plant

Adaptations of xylem to its function

  • The xylem vessels are hollow tubes, which allow for uninterrupted flow of water and minerals.
  • Xylem vessels have open end walls, allowing for the continuous flow of water and minerals from one vessel to another.
  • The walls of tracheids are perforated with pits, which allow for lateral transport of water and minerals between adjacent cells.
  • Xylem vessels are narrow, which increases the ascend of water by capillary action.
  • Lignin and cellulose in wall of xylem increases the adhesion of water and helps the water to rise by capillarity.
  • Lignin in xylem walls also, provides structural support to the plant and prevents the vessels from collapsing under tension of water transport.

Phloem

  • Phloem is also composed of four types of cells, namely sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem fibres and phloem parenchyma.
  • Unlike xylem, all phloem cells are alive at maturity.
  • The main conducting cells in phloem are sieve tubes.
  • Mature sieve tubes are without nucleus, however the remain alive because they are associated with companion cells.
  • The phloem fibres provide structural support to the phloem tissue
  • Phloem parenchyma cells are involved in the storage of organic compounds, including sugars and amino acids

Function of Phloem

  • The main function of phloem is to transport sugars and other organic compounds from where are produced e.g. leaves, to other parts of the plant for use or storage e.g. roots, fruits, etc

Adaptations of phloem to its function of transporting sugars and other organic compounds

  • The sieve tubes are arranged end to end, forming a continuous hollow tube for the transport of organic compounds.
  • The end walls of sieve tubes are perforated with pores forming sieve plates that allow for the flow of organic compounds between adjacent cells.
  • Companion cells provide ATP required for the active transport of organic compounds into sieve tubes.

Differences between Xylem and Phloem

Xylem Phloem
Transports water and minerals Transports sugars and other organic compounds
Dead at maturity Alive at maturity
Conducting cells are tracheids and vessel elements Conducting cells are sieve tubes
Transport water and mineral salts in one direction only (from roots to leaves) Transport organic food substances up and down the plant
Lignin is present in its wall Lignin is absent

Summary

Transport is the process of moving substances from one place to another.

Transport in unicellular organisms is by simple diffusion due to their very small sizes, while in complex multicellular organisms such as plants and animals, a transport system is required to move substances like water, mineral salts, and organic compounds over long distances to meet the metabolic needs of all their body cells.

In plants, the transport system consists of xylem and phloem. Xylem transports water and mineral salts from the roots to the leaves, while phloem transports sugars and other organic compounds from the leaves to other parts of the plant for use or storage.
