Transpiration and wilting

Transpiration

Transpiration is the loss of water in the form of water vapour from the aerial parts of plants, and mainly through the stomata of leaves into the atmosphere. Transpiration can also occur through the cuticle and lenticels

Environmental Factors Affecting the Rate of Transpiration

  • Light intensity: The rate of transpiration increases with increasing light intensity. This is because light stimulates the opening of stomata, allowing more water vapour to escape from the leaves. Decrease in light intensity reduces the rate of transpiration.
  • Temperature: The rate of transpiration increases with increasing temperature. Higher temperatures increase the kinetic energy of water molecules, causing them to evaporate more quickly. Decrease in temperature reduces the rate of transpiration.
  • Humidity: The rate of transpiration decreases with increasing humidity. High humidity reduces the water potential gradient between the inside and outside of the leaf, making it harder for water to evaporate and diffuse out of the leaves. Decrease in humidity increases the rate of transpiration.
  • Wind speed: The rate of transpiration increases with increasing wind speed. Wind removes the layer of humid air around the leaf, increasing the water potential gradient and allowing more water to evaporate and diffuse out of the leaves. Decrease in wind speed reduces the rate of transpiration.
  • Atmospheric pressure: The rate of transpiration decreases with increasing atmospheric pressure. Higher atmospheric pressure increases the density of air, making it harder for water vapour to escape from the leaves. Decrease in atmospheric pressure increases the rate of transpiration.
  • Amount of soil water: The rate of transpiration increases with increasing amount of soil water. More soil water available to the roots increases the water potential gradient, allowing more water to be absorbed and transported to the leaves for transpiration. Decrease in soil water reduces the rate of transpiration.

Non-environmental factors affecting the rate of transpiration

  • Leaf surface area: Larger leaves have more stomata, allowing more water vapour to escape from the leaves, increasing the rate of transpiration. Decrease in leaf surface area reduces the rate of transpiration.
  • Number of stomata: More stomata allow more water vapour to escape from the leaves, increasing the rate of transpiration. Decrease in number of stomata reduces the rate of transpiration.
  • Size of stomatal pores: Wider stomatal openings allow more water vapour to escape from the leaves, increasing the rate of transpiration. Smaller stomatal opening reduces the rate of transpiration.

Importance of transpiration (useful effects)

  • Transpiration helps to cool the leaves as water evaporates from the leaf surface.
  • Transpiration creates suction pressure in the xylem, which helps in the upward movement of water and dissolved minerals from the roots to the leaves.
  • Transpiration helps to maintain the turgidity of plant cells by removing excess water.
  • Transpiration helps to maintain the water potential gradient between the soil and the leaves, allowing for the continuous uptake of water and minerals from the soil.

Harmful effects of transpiration

  • Excessive transpiration can lead to water stress in plants, especially during hot and dry conditions.
  • Excessive transpiration can lead to wilting, which is the loss of turgor pressure in plant cells, causing the leaves and stems to droop.
  • Excessive transpiration can lead to reduced growth and productivity in plants, as the plant may not be able to take up enough water and nutrients to support its metabolic processes.

Wilting

  • Wilting is the loss of turgor pressure (the pressure exerted by the cell contents against the cell wall) in plant cells due to excessive transpiration.
  • Wilting occurs when the plant loses more water into the atmosphere than it can absorb from the soil. The cells of the leaves become flaccid and the leaves become limp and droop.
  • Factors which promote wilting include: high temperature, low humidity, and strong winds and damage to the root system.

Plant wilting

Adaptations of plants to reduce water loss by transpiration

  • Some plants have a thick cuticle that reduces the rate of water loss through the leaf surface.
  • Some plants close their stomata during the day to reduce the rate of water loss through transpiration.
  • Some plants have smaller leaves that have less surface area for water loss.
  • Some plants have a waxy coating on the leaf surface that can reduce water loss.
  • Some plants have sunken stomata that reduce the rate of water loss through transpiration.
  • Some plants have hairs on the leaf surface to trap a layer of moist air, thus creating a humid microclimate around the leaf, reducing water loss.
  • Some plants roll their leaves to reduce the surface area exposed to the sun and wind, thus reducing water loss.
  • Some plants shed their leaves during dry seasons to reduce water loss. Such plants are called deciduous plants.

Guttation

  • Guttation is the loss of water in the form of droplets from the edges of leaves through structures called hydathodes.
  • Guttation occurs when the soil is very moist and the rate of absorption of water by the roots is greater than the rate of transpiration.
  • Guttation is usually observed in the early morning when the humidity is high and the stomata are closed.
  • Water lost through guttation contains minerals and other substances dissolved in it.

Guttation

Similarities between Transpiration and Guttation

  • Both processes involve water loss from plants.
  • Both processes are influenced by environmental factors such as temperature and humidity.
  • Both processes occur through the leaves.
  • Both processes occur in plants.

Differences between Transpiration and Guttation

Summary

Transpiration is the loss of water in the form of water vapour from the aerial parts of plants, through the stomata of leaves, cuticle and lenticels into the atmosphere.

The rate of transpiration is affected by environmental factors such as light intensity, temperature, humidity, wind speed, atmospheric pressure and amount of soil water. Non-environmental factors such as leaf surface area, number of stomata and size of stomatal pores also affect the rate of transpiration.

Plants have various adaptations to reduce water loss by transpiration, such as thick cuticles, closing stomata during the day, smaller leaves, waxy coatings, sunken stomata, leaf hairs, rolling leaves and shedding leaves during dry seasons.

Transpiration has both useful and harmful effects on plants. It helps to cool the leaves, create suction pressure in the xylem, maintain turgidity of plant cells and maintain the water potential gradient between the soil and the leaves. However, excessive transpiration can lead to water stress, wilting and reduced growth and productivity in plants.

Guttation is the loss of water in the form of droplets from the edges of leaves through hydathodes. It is mainly driven by root pressure, and is favoured by low temperature and high humidity.

Wilting is the loss of turgor pressure in plant cells due to excessive transpiration. It can result in poor growth, reduced productivity in plants and even death.
