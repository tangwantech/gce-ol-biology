Experiments on transport in plants

Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:

  • Demonstrate that xylem conducts water from the roots to the leaves.
  • Demonstrate transpiration in plants.
  • Measure the rate of transpiration in a leafy shoot.
  • Measure the rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot using a potometer.

Experiment 1

Aim: To demonstrate that xylem conduct water from the roots to the leaves

Requirements:

Herbaceous plant, Beaker, eosin dye, razor blade and light microscope.

Procedure:

  • A medium sized herbaceous plant e.g. balsam is uprooted, and the roots are washed in tap water.
  • The plant is placed in a beaker containing eosin solution (red dye). The roots must be completely covered by the solution.
  • Allow the setup for about 3 hours.
  • At the end of three hours, the plant is taken out of the solution and its roots washed in tap water.
  • Transverse sections are made of the roots, stem, branches and leaves. These are examined under a light microscope.

Result:

The xylem vessels are stained red indicating that water has been conducted through them in different organs.

Conclusion:

Xylem conducts water from the roots to the leaves.

Experiment 2

Aim: To demonstrate transpiration in plants

Requirements:

Potted plant, polythene bag, bell jar, glass plate and vaseline

Procedure:

Experiment to demonstrate transpiration in plants

  • Wrap a polythene bag around the pot and stem of a well watered potted plant. This prevents loss of water by evaporation from the soil surface.
  • Place the pot on a glass plate and cover it with a dry bell jar. Label the experiment A. This serves as the test experiment.
  • Setup a similar experiment labelled B, but without a plant. This serves as the control experiment.
  • Place the two bell jars under similar environemental conditions for two hours.

Result

  • After two hours, water will would be seen to have condensed on the inner wall of the bell jar containing the potted plant (test experiment).
  • On the other hand, no water has condensed in the bell jar without a potted plant.

Conclusion:

Transpiration takes place in plants.

Experiment 3

Aim: To measure the rate of transpiration in a leafy shoot

Requirements:

Oil, boiling tube, water, leafy shoot, and sensitive spring balance.

Procedure:

Experiment to measure the rate of transpiration in a leafy shoot.

  • Cut a leafy shoot under water to prevent air bubbles from entering the xylem.
  • Immerse the cut end in a test tube of water and add a little oil on the surface to prevent evaporation.
  • Tie a string around the test tube and weigh on a delicate spring balance. Record the mass.
  • Place the test tube in a test tube rack near a window.
  • After about 3 hours, weigh the test tube and the shoot again. Record the new mass.

Result:

The weight of the test tube and leafy shoot is observed to have dropped. The loss in weight is due to transpiration.

The rate of transpiration is calculated as follows:

Mass of tube with shoot before 3 hours = X (g)

Mass of tube with shoot after 3 hours = Y (g)

Rate of transpiration = Loss of mass in grams / Duration in hours

= (Xg - Yg) / 3 hours

Experiment 4 (Potometer experiment)

Aim: To measure the rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot

Requirements:

Potometer, leafy shoot.

Procedure:

Experiment to measure the rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot.

  • Cut a leafy shoot under water and keep the cut end in water for a few hours before use. This ensures that air bubbles do not enter the xylem vessels.
  • Insert the shoot through the hole in the cork of the potometer.
  • Smear vaseline around the region of the shoot that passes through the cork to make it airtight.
  • Open the tap of the reservoir to fill the graduated capillary tube with water. Close the tap when the tube is full.
  • Introduce an air bubble into the capillary tube by opening the tap of the reservoir.
  • Record the initial position of the air bubble in the capillary tube (point A).
  • Allow the setup to stand for a period of time and record the final position of the air bubble in the capillary tube (point B).

Result:

  • As the shoot transpires, it absorbs water from the potometer to replace that lost during transpiration. This causes the water column and air bubble in the capillary tube to move from B to A.
  • The rate of movement of the bubble gives the rate of absorption or water uptake by the shoot.

Rate of water uptake = Distance moved by air bubble / time taken to move from B to A
